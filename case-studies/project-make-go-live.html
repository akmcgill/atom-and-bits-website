Kroger · Manufacturing · Project MAKE

The champions who caught what testing couldn't

As Project MAKE approached go-live, the most tenured plant associates resisted the new system more than new hires did — the opposite of what the team expected. A listening session revealed why, and a peer-led "champions" network built on that insight carried the rollout to zero plant downtime.

The problem

During Project MAKE, tenured plant associates resisted the new ERP system more than new hires did — counterintuitive, given how disliked the legacy system already was. The pattern showed up across multiple facilities, and it didn't fit the usual explanation of "resistance to change" for its own sake.

A listening session with a 20-year plant associate reframed the entire problem (see her words below). She wasn't afraid of learning something new — she was afraid of losing the standing she'd built over a career. The same pattern repeated across interviews: expertise on the floor lived in knowing "the right person to talk to," and a well-designed system risked erasing that identity entirely.

💬
Why this stalled

A top-down training rollout would have solved the wrong problem. The resistance wasn't about capability — it was about status, and no amount of better documentation was going to fix that.

"I take pride in knowing the system. I'm the go-to person when people have questions. If you take away the old system, I'm on the same level as someone who just walked off the street."

— A 20-year plant associate, in a pre-go-live listening session

What I did

The response was a peer-led model, not a top-down one: embed a small team of tenured "MAKE Champions" directly with the project through testing, training, and go-live — advocating for the new system in terms that resonated with their peers, not the language executives would use.

1

Listening sessions

Surfaced the real, identity-driven reason behind frontline resistance — not assumed, not guessed, but heard directly from the associates living it.

2

Peer-led champion network

Recruited tenured associates whose floor credibility couldn't be replicated by a manager or a training deck, and gave them a real role in the rollout.

3

Embedded through go-live

Champions stayed with the project through testing, training, and go-live itself — not a one-time kickoff session, but sustained presence at every stage that mattered.

The champions weren't a communications layer bolted on top of the rollout — they were inside it. Because they were testing and training alongside the project team, they caught real errors before those errors ever reached a live plant floor.

“
We feel like someone is finally listening to us.
— A plant associate, after go-live

Outcome

The champions caught errors before they hit production, and the rollout went live across the program with zero plant downtime. Just as important, the champions model shifted how the floor felt about the whole project — not something done to them, but something they'd had a real hand in shaping.
