The problem

The objectives were clear: reduce the cost of new-associate onboarding and cut help-desk tickets tied to training gaps. What was ambiguous was everything underneath that. As the underlying inventory app, In Stock, scaled from roughly 800 to more than 2,000 stores, new retail associates had one day to learn to manage inventory on an Android handheld device. Training time was limited and often incomplete. Roughly 28% of help-desk tickets for that device and app traced back to issues basic training should have already covered — but nobody had defined where the real failure point was, or what to build to fix it.

"Someone showed me how to hack the system so it looks like I'm counting. I don't even get why we're doing this."

— A store associate, describing a workaround for the new process
Training lived in a conference room, disconnected from the floor Legacy training model

"We don't really have the time or the budget to round people into the conference room for training. Everybody works different schedules and no one really uses the training computers." — a department lead, on why in-person sessions kept slipping.

New associates got the device with no context for why anything changed Onboarding gap

"I was just handed the device and was told it replaces the RF Handhelds. I was used to doing it the old way. Not really sure why they changed things." — a newly onboarded associate.

Teams that understood the "why" adopted the process fully Early signal

"We were one of the pilot teams so we've had more time to work the process. Now we're seeing the results and there's really no denying how things have improved." — an associate on an early pilot team.

Help desk was absorbing problems that weren't actually technical Support cost

"So many of the issues getting reported are not even tech related. People don't have a good baseline understanding of what's different so they blame it on the technology." — tech support, on the ticket volume that eventually confirmed the 28% baseline.

🔍
Finding the real failure point

Analyzed help-desk tickets with Copilot to surface themes and sentiment, then interviewed new associates, department leads, store captains, and the help-desk team — and did floor-level UX research watching associates actually use the device, instead of guessing where an intervention would matter.

What I did

That research shaped the metric: associates needed microtraining delivered at the moment they hit a problem, not before, paired with a measurable drop in tickets. It also ruled out the obvious first answer — revising onboarding curriculum — because interviews showed in-person training wasn't reliably delivered store to store. An embedded, in-app layer was the only approach that could guarantee the same training quality regardless of which store or trainer someone got. The design followed three rules: start with the why, so associates could build a real mental model of the new system instead of just complying with it; make it accessible from inside the device itself, so anyone could refer back to it any time; and build it to perform in actual store conditions — low bandwidth, no dependence on video — not a conference-room demo.

Why in-app, not onboarding

In-person training wasn't guaranteed and varied significantly store to store. Improving a curriculum that wasn't reliably delivered in the first place wouldn't have solved the problem — the training had to live inside the workflow itself.

The team prototyped an in-app training and support layer and tested it onsite with store associates before building further — validating the approach against real behavior on the floor rather than a conference-room assumption.

“
Formal training was removed from onboarding entirely — the in-app layer wasn't a supplement to what wasn't working. It replaced it.
— Karina, on the outcome of the platform's first version

Outcome

The first version reached 300,000 retail associates and third-party vendors. Formal training was removed from onboarding entirely, and help-desk tickets dropped 25% in the first quarter. The result also changed how the business saw in-app learning: it led to a follow-on pilot of Axonify, a third-party AI-driven microlearning platform, which Karina also owned.
